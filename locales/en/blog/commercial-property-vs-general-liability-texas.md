Business owners often hear the phrases “commercial property” and “general liability” together. They may appear on one proposal, and both can be important. But they address different questions. **Commercial property generally concerns qualifying physical loss to insured business property; commercial general liability generally concerns certain claims from others that the business is legally responsible for injury or damage.**

Understanding the distinction helps a business owner avoid assuming that one insurance card or certificate protects every building, person, and piece of equipment.

## Commercial property focuses on the business's insured property

Depending on the form, commercial property insurance may help repair or replace an insured building, eligible inventory, furniture, or equipment after a covered cause of loss. TDI explains that policies may use basic, broad, or special causes-of-loss forms and may include or offer related business income and extra expense options.

The policy's location schedule, property definitions, valuation basis, deductibles, and exclusions control. A building owner and [a commercial tenant](/blog/commercial-property-insurance-business-tenants-texas/) often have different property interests. An owner's policy may cover the structure but not all of the tenant's stock.

## General liability focuses on certain third-party allegations

TDI describes commercial general liability (CGL) as protection against covered liability claims for bodily injury, property damage, and certain personal and advertising injuries. Premises and operations coverage may be relevant if a customer is injured on the insured premises or a business activity damages another person's property, subject to the insurer's contract and legal responsibility.

General liability is not designed as ordinary first-party coverage for the insured's building or inventory. It also may exclude or restrict professional errors, auto accidents, contractual promises, or other exposures that could call for separate coverage.

| Situation | Insurance category to review | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fire damages the insured shop's own roof | Commercial property | Damage to the insured's physical building |
| Hail damages the business's covered inventory storage structure | Commercial property | Covered property and cause of loss must be established |
| Customer slips and alleges injury on the shop floor | General liability | Potential third-party bodily injury claim |
| Employee accidentally damages a customer's property while working | General liability or other specialized protection | Liability depends on activity and policy exclusions |
| Owner's own laptop wears out from age | Neither automatically | Wear/maintenance and first-party property exclusions may apply |
| Damaged building forces temporary closure | Business income option, if purchased | Lost income requires its own covered trigger and terms |

This table is educational. No example is a claim approval decision.

## A small Texas business may need to evaluate both

*Hypothetical scenario:* An El Paso coffee shop rents its location. A covered fire damages the shop's furniture and merchandise. That raises a first-party property question. Separately, a visitor alleges they were injured because of an unsafe floor condition before the fire. That raises a third-party liability question. The two events involve different insurance sections and evidence, even if the business has a BOP containing both types of coverage.

The landlord may also have its own building insurance, and the tenant may have responsibilities under the lease. Neither party should assume the other party's policy replaces its own needs.

## What is a BOP, and does it solve the problem?

A business owners policy, or BOP, is a package that may combine eligible property and liability protection. TDI discusses BOPs as a way many businesses can buy combined coverage. However, eligibility, covered buildings, business types, sublimits, deductibles, and endorsements vary. A BOP is not a universal promise to cover every risk.

For example, a BOP may still have flood exclusions, restrictions on off-premises tools, limited cyber coverage, or other gaps. It also may not meet a lender's or customer's contractual requirements. Compare the actual coverage parts as carefully as standalone policies.

## Professional services and specialized risks

An accountant, architect, consultant, or other professional may own an office and provide services to clients. Property insurance addresses specified property risks. General liability addresses certain third-party injury or property damage allegations. A professional's alleged mistake in providing services can be a different professional liability or errors-and-omissions exposure, which is not automatically covered under ordinary property or general liability.

This distinction matters for a business that occupies only a small office: insuring office contents is not the same as insuring advice, contracts, or professional judgments. Ask which policies address each real activity and review exclusions.

## Why contracts and certificates require attention

A landlord may ask a tenant for general liability coverage and proof of insurance. A lender may ask a building owner for property insurance with appropriate mortgagee wording. These requests concern different exposures. Certificates summarize policy information and do not create broader protection than the issued forms.

Texas has rules governing the content of certificates of insurance, explained in TDI's official FAQ. If a contract asks for an endorsement, seek confirmation that the insurer has actually provided it before telling another party that the requirement is satisfied.

## A five-question coverage review

1. Who owns the building, equipment, and inventory?
2. Which property could be damaged, and by what causes?
3. Who could claim that the business injured them or damaged their property?
4. What professional, auto, cyber, flood, or other exposures are separate?
5. What does each lease, lender agreement, or client contract require, and do the actual policies meet it?

Keeping the questions separate makes insurance proposals easier to understand and can reveal limits or exclusions that a broad marketing summary leaves unclear.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does general liability cover my building after a fire?

Do not assume so. Insurance for the business's own covered building generally belongs to commercial property, while CGL concerns certain liability claims from others.

### Does commercial property pay when a customer is hurt?

Not automatically. A third-party injury allegation is generally a general-liability question, subject to legal responsibility and contract wording.

### If I buy a BOP, is everything insured?

No. A BOP combines specified coverage parts but still has eligibility requirements, limits, deductibles, and exclusions.
