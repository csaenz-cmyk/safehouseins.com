Many small businesses do not own the building where they operate. They rent a retail unit, office suite, salon, café, or warehouse. It is easy to assume that the landlord's insurance protects the entire space. **In reality, the landlord and tenant commonly have different property interests and insurance responsibilities.**

TDI specifically explains that a commercial building owner's policy does not automatically protect a tenant from loss. A tenant may need insurance for furniture, inventory, machinery, and other business contents. The exact division depends on the lease, ownership of the improvements, policy definitions, and loss circumstances.

## Start with what you actually own

Walk through the premises and list property the business purchased or is responsible for replacing. That may include chairs, desks, computers, point-of-sale systems, refrigerators, display racks, specialized workstations, merchandise, supplies, and certain permanently installed improvements. Identify who owns each major item and where it is normally kept.

Do not automatically place the entire building's reconstruction value on a tenant's contents quote. Conversely, do not omit substantial tenant-paid improvements because they are attached to a leased space. Some improvements and betterments may require specific coverage wording.

| Property or expense | Party or policy question |
|---|---|
| Roof, exterior walls, structural building | Typically a building-owner interest, subject to lease and policy |
| Tenant-owned furniture and computers | Tenant business personal property coverage |
| Inventory held for sale | Tenant stock and valuation; seasonal changes matter |
| Counter or fixtures installed at tenant expense | Improvements and betterments treatment |
| Customer items held by the tenant | Potential special property-in-care or inland marine coverage |
| Tenant's lost income after covered damage | Business income coverage if included and triggered |
| Injury allegation from a visitor | Separate commercial general liability review |

The table is a starting point; the lease and issued insurance contracts must be checked.

## Read the lease before buying insurance

A commercial lease may require a tenant to maintain [general liability](/blog/commercial-property-vs-general-liability-texas/), insure its contents, name specified parties where appropriate, pay for certain improvements, or provide certificates. These obligations are contractual and can differ from one landlord to another. Insurance availability and policy conditions are separate from what the lease asks for.

Ask who is responsible for storefront glass, interior partitions, plumbing fixtures, HVAC equipment, appliances, and outdoor signs. Some leases assign maintenance duties that are not identical to insurable property interests. Even if the tenant is required to maintain or repair an item, a particular insurance policy may not automatically respond to every loss.

TDI's FAQ also explains limitations on what an insurance certificate may say. A certificate does not amend an underlying policy or create an endorsement; request the actual policy language where necessary.

## An El Paso example: leased salon space

*Hypothetical scenario:* A stylist signs a lease for a small salon in an El Paso plaza. The landlord owns the building, but the business pays for styling chairs, cabinetry, mirrors, computers, and specialty equipment. The business also makes tenant improvements. If a covered fire damages the space, building repairs and tenant-owned contents are separate questions. A lease clause requiring the tenant to repair certain items does not by itself guarantee those items are insured.

Before opening, the stylist should compare the lease, property inventory, and proposed insurance, then ask about improvements, general liability, loss of income, and any special glass coverage.

## How much contents coverage should a tenant consider?

Start with an actual inventory rather than guessing based on rent. Add the current value of office furniture, machinery, equipment, inventory, and supplies using the insurer's requested valuation approach. Consider seasonal stock, replacement costs, and expensive items that may have special limits. This is a valuation exercise, not a direction to buy any fixed amount.

*Hypothetical arithmetic:* A tenant lists $16,000 in machinery, $9,000 in furnishings, and $25,000 in merchandise. The initial total is **$50,000** of items to evaluate. Additional fixtures, improvements, exclusions, or inventory fluctuations could change the appropriate requested limit and what the insurer will accept.

## Business income after a building loss

Even if the landlord repairs the building, the tenant may be unable to conduct business for a period. Business income and extra expense provisions, when included, may be relevant after qualifying covered physical damage. Ask whether coverage is based on damage at the insured premises and how the insured's own property versus landlord property affects the trigger. Waiting periods, restoration windows, and policy conditions are important.

If the business simply loses customers without covered property damage, the ordinary business income provision may not respond. Do not confuse ongoing rent obligations with guaranteed insurance payments.

## Relocations, multiple sites, and property in transit

Tenants sometimes move between suites, open a second location, or store stock off-site. The policy's location schedule matters. A business policy covering property at one described address should not be assumed to cover a new unit automatically. TDI discusses inland marine and other options for certain property in transit or off-premises; ask about equipment that regularly travels.

Notify the agency before a move, occupancy change, or renovation when possible. Review when any existing coverage ends and when the new location's coverage begins. Avoid gaps created by waiting until after opening to update the insurer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does my landlord's insurance cover my inventory?

Not automatically. TDI explains that building owners' coverage generally does not protect tenant contents just because they are inside the building.

### Does my lease requiring insurance mean any policy will satisfy it?

No. Compare the lease's actual insurance requirements with the policy and endorsements the insurer is willing to issue.

### If the landlord owns the HVAC system, can my policy cover it?

It depends on ownership, lease responsibilities, policy definitions and any special provisions. Ask before assuming either party's insurer will respond.
