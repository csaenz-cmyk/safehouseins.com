If you drive in New Mexico, one of the first auto insurance questions you may ask is, “How much coverage does the state require?” The answer is important, but so is understanding what those numbers actually pay for—and what they do not. A policy can meet a state minimum while still leaving a driver responsible for costs that exceed the policy's limits.

New Mexico's Motor Vehicle Division (MVD) lists the following minimum limits for an ordinary auto liability policy. Together they are commonly called **25/50/10**. These are limits for covered legal responsibility to *other people*, not an amount reserved to repair your own car.

| Liability limit | New Mexico minimum | What the amount limits |
|---|---:|---|
| Bodily injury, one person | **$25,000** | Eligible injury or death claims for one person in one accident |
| Bodily injury, one accident | **$50,000** | Eligible injury or death claims for all people combined in one accident |
| Property damage, one accident | **$10,000** | Covered damage to other people's property in one accident |

The MVD also discusses limited alternatives to an insurance policy for establishing financial responsibility. For ordinary shopping comparisons, drivers usually evaluate a liability policy and its stated limits.

## What does 25/50/10 mean in everyday language?

The first $25,000 is the most the policy may pay under its bodily injury liability limit for **one injured person** in a covered accident, subject to the policy and the facts. The $50,000 is the total bodily injury liability limit for **all injured people combined** in a single covered accident. These two figures work together; $50,000 does not mean every injured person gets $50,000.

The final $10,000 is a separate limit for property damage liability in one accident. It may help pay for covered damage to another person's car, fence, building, or other property when you are legally responsible. It generally does not pay to repair your own vehicle after an at-fault collision.

### A numerical example of a limit

Imagine an entirely **hypothetical** covered crash in which a New Mexico driver is legally responsible. Two people in the other vehicle have eligible injury losses of $40,000 and $35,000, and the other car has $16,000 in eligible property damage. Under a 25/50/10 policy, the injury liability limit for *either individual* is $25,000. The maximum for both injured people combined is $50,000, not $75,000. The property damage liability maximum is $10,000. Even when a loss qualifies, amounts above the relevant limits may remain the responsible driver's problem.

Actual claims are subject to fault, the policy's definitions and limits, exclusions, settlements, and applicable law. This illustration shows the arithmetic of limits only; it is not a prediction of claim approval or payment.

## Is the minimum the same as enough coverage?

Not necessarily. A state minimum is a legal starting point, not a statement that the amount fits every driver's finances. Repair costs for some newer vehicles may exceed $10,000, and an accident involving more than one injured person can become expensive. The policy's limits apply even if a claim is larger.

You can ask for quotes showing different liability limits and compare the premium difference. A higher limit may provide more available liability protection for a covered loss, but it still cannot eliminate every financial risk. Think about the vehicles you drive near, how frequently you drive, and how much responsibility you would be able to handle after a serious accident.

## Does minimum liability cover your own car?

Typically, no. Liability insurance is designed primarily around covered harm you cause to others. To address certain losses to your own insured vehicle, you would need to consider **collision** and **comprehensive** coverage, if available and appropriate.

Collision generally concerns a covered crash involving your own vehicle. Comprehensive generally concerns covered non-collision losses such as theft, hail, certain falling objects, or vandalism. Both usually have deductibles. Neither is a blanket promise that every situation is covered. If you finance or lease a vehicle, your agreement may require these coverages even though ordinary state liability law does not.

## What about a driver who has no insurance?

Uninsured and underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage is another topic to discuss separately. New Mexico has legal requirements surrounding the offer and rejection of this protection. Do not assume that having the minimum liability limits means you bought UM/UIM coverage, or that a policy necessarily covers every hit-and-run situation. Check your selections, policy language, and any signed rejection documents.

## What proof of insurance should you keep?

The MVD lists a current insurance card, current policy copy, or insurer letter as examples of proof when registering a vehicle. New Mexico also uses an electronic **Insurance Identification Database (IIDB)** to match active insurance information to registered vehicles. A correct VIN and accurate insurer reporting matter. If you receive a noncompliance notice despite having insurance, contact your company promptly and follow the instructions; do not assume the notice will resolve itself.

Drivers moving into New Mexico should check insurance, license, title, and registration steps together. An out-of-state card alone does not establish that every MVD requirement has been handled for your particular circumstances.

## Questions worth asking before buying

- Are these my exact bodily injury and property damage liability limits?
- How would the policy respond if several people were injured in one accident?
- What coverage, if any, protects my own car from a crash, hail, or theft?
- Are uninsured/underinsured motorist options included, offered, or rejected?
- Do the named drivers, garaging address, vehicle identification number, and uses match reality?

The best insurance conversation is not simply “What is the cheapest policy that lets me drive?” It is “What protection am I actually buying, what could remain my responsibility, and what does the policy say?”
