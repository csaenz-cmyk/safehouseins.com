Two homeowners insurance proposals may protect the same address but leave the owner with very different expenses after a loss. One has a lower annual premium. Another uses a different dwelling amount, roof valuation method, or hail deductible. If you compare only the total due, you may overlook the very differences you wanted insurance to address.

The best comparison starts with **matching facts and identifying meaningful differences**. TDI's shopping resources recommend reviewing the coverages, replacement cost, deductibles, optional protections, and insurer information—not just price.

## Begin with accurate, consistent property information

Give each insurer the same truthful details: address, year built, square footage, construction, roof age and material, occupancy, prior losses where requested, renovations, presence of pools or other exposures, and intended use. A home that is owner-occupied all year may be evaluated differently from a rental or a property left vacant for extended periods.

If one proposal assumes a roof is five years old and another assumes it is fifteen, resolve the discrepancy. If one quote treats the residence as a primary home and the other labels it seasonal, neither price comparison nor coverage assumptions may be reliable.

## Compare dwelling limits and replacement-cost estimates

Dwelling coverage should be examined in light of estimated rebuilding expenses. Two companies may use different estimating tools or ask different questions. If the resulting limits vary, ask which construction details explain the difference.

Do not automatically choose the lowest dwelling amount. Conversely, a large limit is not proof that the policy will cover every repair. Compare attached structures, building ordinance or law provisions, special limits, and how the insurer settles qualifying losses.

## Roof terms deserve their own line in your comparison

Find out if the roof is settled at replacement cost or ACV. Review any age-related roof schedule, cosmetic-damage restriction, inspection requirement, or wind/hail exclusion. An annual premium difference can be less meaningful than the size of an uncovered roof replacement expense.

Be particularly careful when a proposal summary displays “replacement cost” near the dwelling limit; the roof endorsement may contain its own conditions. Ask to see the written wording, not a salesperson's casual description.

## Convert every deductible into dollars

If the dwelling amount is $300,000, a 1% deductible is $3,000 and a 2% deductible is $6,000 under a straightforward percentage-of-dwelling calculation. A quote with a lower premium and double the applicable hail deductible is not equivalent to another quote.

Check separate deductibles for wind/hail, tropical storm or hurricane where applicable, other property losses, and any special endorsements. Make sure the policy's percentage base is correctly understood.

## A fictional comparison worksheet

The following amounts are **entirely hypothetical** and are not carrier quotes, Safe House Insurance rates, statewide averages, savings promises, or offers of coverage. The purpose is to show why an apparent price advantage may not answer the homeowner's main question.

| Feature | Hypothetical Proposal A | Hypothetical Proposal B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Annual premium | $2,300 | $2,000 |
| Dwelling limit | $300,000 | $300,000 |
| Roof settlement | Replacement cost, subject to terms | Actual cash value |
| Wind/hail deductible | 1% = $3,000 | 2% = $6,000 |
| Personal liability limit | $300,000 | $100,000 |
| Additional living expenses | Review policy limit/terms | Review policy limit/terms |
| Water-backup endorsement | Not stated; must check | Not stated; must check |

Proposal B is $300 less in this **invented annual example**, but it also shows materially different risk. That does not automatically make Proposal A the right choice. Actual premiums, endorsements, eligibility, and company terms require individual evaluation. Nor does the table imply either proposed combination is currently sold.

## Check belongings, structures, and living expenses

Compare limits for personal property, detached structures, and additional living expenses. Two policies with the same dwelling limit may still differ in contents valuation, jewelry sublimits, payment conditions, or temporary living arrangements after a covered loss.

If the homeowner has a detached garage used for work, a substantial jewelry collection, or specialized equipment, ask how those features are categorized and whether additional terms are needed. Do not assume ordinary personal property coverage addresses business stock or every high-value item.

## Read exclusions and endorsements, not just declarations

The declarations page summarizes important information, but endorsements can change it. Review water damage, mold, water-backup, roof ACV, cosmetic damage, foundation, vacancy, animal-related liability, and any other provision material to the property.

Be cautious of comparing “all risk” and “named peril” forms as though the labels alone tell you the result of every claim. The covered property, insuring agreement, exclusions, limitations, and endorsements all matter. Ask which differences would actually affect scenarios you are concerned about.

## Consider the insurer and the process

Premium is not the only practical criterion. Ask whether the quote is preliminary or ready for underwriting, what inspections or documentation remain, how payments are handled, and when coverage could begin after a company accepts an application.

You can examine publicly available insurer licensing, complaint resources, and financial-strength information using appropriate sources, while understanding that no public indicator guarantees a favorable future claim result. An independent agency can help organize the proposals; it cannot override an insurer's underwriting decisions.

## Avoid accidental gaps when switching

A quote is not a policy. Before canceling existing coverage, confirm that the replacement policy has actually been issued, the effective date and time, any payment requirements, and whether the lender received adequate proof. If the new company requires an inspection or final verification, find out whether that affects binding or subsequent eligibility.

For a mortgaged property, coordinate the mortgagee clause and escrow billing if relevant. A lender may have its own insurance documentation requirements, but satisfying the lender does not necessarily mean the homeowner has chosen all the coverages they would want.

## Your six-line quote review

1. Same property and occupancy information?
2. Comparable dwelling reconstruction limits and contents protection?
3. Same roof valuation and relevant exclusions?
4. Every deductible converted into dollars?
5. Same liability and living-expense limits, including important endorsements?
6. Total premium, payment schedule, carrier status, and effective date confirmed?
