A Texas homeowners policy can address more than repairs after a covered fire. It is a collection of separate coverages, each designed for a different kind of property, expense, or responsibility. Understanding those parts matters because two policies can both be called “homeowners insurance” and still differ in exclusions, deductibles, claim payments, and coverage limits.

The Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) explains that homeowners policies commonly contain protection for the home itself, personal belongings, other structures, additional living expenses, personal liability, and medical payments to others. But **the policy you actually buy**, including endorsements and exclusions, is what determines whether a particular loss qualifies.

## 1. Dwelling coverage: the structure of your house

Dwelling coverage generally addresses damage to the residence from events covered by the contract. Depending on policy form and endorsements, covered causes might include fire, lightning, some wind or hail losses, and certain other sudden events. It is not a guarantee of payment for everything that can happen to a building.

The insured dwelling amount should relate to the estimated cost to **rebuild or repair** the home—not merely what you paid for the property or what a real estate website says it could sell for. Land value, market demand, and rebuilding labor costs are different considerations. A major fire can illustrate why a home's sale price and reconstruction expense need not be equal.

Check which attached structures and permanent fixtures count as part of the dwelling. Ask about foundation, cosmetic-damage, and roof-related conditions if those are material to the house.

## 2. Other structures: fences, detached garages, and sheds

A detached garage, freestanding shed, or fence may fall under a separate other-structures section. Its limit can be expressed as a percentage of the dwelling limit, but that percentage is not identical on every form. A structure used partly for business or rented to someone else may raise additional eligibility or coverage questions.

For example, a detached workshop containing tools for a paid contracting business should not automatically be treated like a backyard shed holding garden supplies. Tell the insurer how the building is actually used, rather than expecting an ordinary home quote to address every commercial exposure.

## 3. Personal property: the things you own inside

Furniture, clothing, appliances, electronics, and other possessions can be insured under personal-property coverage, but limits, sublimits, and valuation methods matter. Jewelry, art, collectibles, business property, and certain expensive devices may have special limits or require additional scheduling or endorsements.

The phrase “my belongings are covered” does not specify whether payment would be based on **replacement cost** or **actual cash value**, and it does not answer whether a listed type of loss is insured. Create a simple home inventory with photos, purchase information when available, serial numbers for important devices, and a safe off-site copy. It is much easier to remember what was in a room before a loss than afterward.

## 4. Additional living expenses: when you cannot stay at home

If a covered loss makes the residence temporarily uninhabitable, additional living expenses (ALE), sometimes called loss-of-use coverage, may help with qualifying extra costs during a covered period. Potential examples include certain temporary lodging and the added cost of meals beyond normal spending, subject to the contract and documentation.

It is not a general relocation allowance. A voluntary hotel stay because repairs are inconvenient, or displacement from a cause excluded by the policy, might not qualify. Review the dollar or time limit and ask what documentation the insurer would need if temporary housing became necessary.

## 5. Liability and medical payments to others

Personal liability coverage can address certain allegations that you are legally responsible for someone else's injury or property damage. The policy may also include a separate, usually smaller, medical-payments-to-others provision. These are **features of homeowners insurance**, not an assurance that every lawsuit will be defended or paid.

Imagine a hypothetical visitor slipping on a hazardous step. Whether a liability claim is covered depends on the actual facts, legal responsibility, exclusions, and the applicable policy. The presence of a pool, certain animals, frequent paid guests, or business activity may change the risk and should be accurately disclosed.

## What is usually outside an ordinary homeowners policy?

TDI lists common exclusions or limitations that homeowners should discuss before purchase. These may include flooding, earthquakes or earth movement, wear and tear, pest damage, gradual deterioration, certain mold situations, and losses involving a vacant residence. Some protections can be added by endorsement or separate policy; others may not be available for a particular risk.

| Situation | Question to ask before buying |
|---|---|
| Fire damages the kitchen | Is fire a covered cause, and what dwelling deductible applies? |
| Wind damages roof shingles | Does this address have wind/hail coverage, and what is the roof settlement basis? |
| Rising outside water enters the house | Do I need a separate flood policy? |
| A pipe slowly leaks for months | How does the policy treat seepage, mold, and long-term damage? |
| A visitor alleges an injury | What liability limits and exclusions apply? |
| A detached workshop is damaged | Is the structure's actual use eligible under this policy? |

The table is a checklist, **not a coverage determination**. Each answer depends on the policy and the event.

## Texas is not one uniform insurance market

An El Paso house and a Gulf Coast house may face different weather exposures and policy arrangements. For example, coastal homeowners might need to examine separate wind and hail insurance, while a homeowner elsewhere may find wind included but subject to a higher deductible. A flood policy is a separate question even when the regular home policy addresses rain entering through a covered storm-created opening.

Avoid assuming that a neighbor's policy, an online example, or the phrase “standard Texas coverage” describes yours. Look at the declarations page, endorsements, and exclusions together.

## A useful declarations-page check

Before choosing a policy, verify the named insured, property address, occupancy, dwelling limit, other-structures limit, contents limit, loss-of-use limit, liability limit, and each deductible. Then read the pages that explain excluded causes of loss and special roof or water conditions. The first page alone rarely tells the whole story.

If the home has solar panels, an attached patio enclosure, expensive equipment, or a separate structure, ask whether the application and insurer's valuation describe those features correctly. If you have a mortgage, confirm the lender's insurance requirements as well.
