Two property insurance proposals can show [different premiums](/blog/commercial-property-insurance-cost-texas/) for reasons that are not obvious on the first page. One may use replacement cost for a building; another may settle roof damage at actual cash value. One may include business income; another may omit it. Wind and hail deductibles, flood exclusions, property limits, and occupancy assumptions can also differ.

To compare the proposals fairly, review the insured building and contents, causes of loss, valuation basis, deductibles, exclusions, business income options, and full policy cost. The goal is not to select the lowest number automatically, but to understand **what each policy would actually insure under its written terms** and how much financial responsibility the business keeps if a covered loss occurs.

## Step 1: Make the property descriptions identical

Give every insurer the same accurate information: full property address, owner or tenant status, square footage, construction, roof facts, use and occupancy, fire protection, building values, contents inventory, and business operations. If the property has multiple tenants, detached structures, off-site stock, or renovations, include them consistently.

A quote based on an empty warehouse is not comparable with one based on an active restaurant in that warehouse. Correct incomplete applications before drawing conclusions about prices or eligibility.

## Step 2: Check who and what is insured

Verify the legal business name, property owner, tenants if applicable, mortgagees or loss payees, and listed addresses. Confirm building coverage, business personal property, tenant improvements, outdoor property, and any special equipment coverage separately. If a quote excludes a detached storage building, ask about it before assuming the second proposal is more expensive for no reason.

TDI notes that commercial property insurance forms are not standardized in Texas. Equivalent-looking proposal headings are not proof that the underlying forms match.

## Step 3: Compare causes of loss and valuation

Ask whether the quote uses a basic, broad, or special causes-of-loss form or a carrier-specific alternative. Review exclusions and deductibles for fire, smoke, wind, hail, roof, theft, water, and flood. Then compare replacement cost versus ACV, any insurance-to-value or coinsurance language, and conditions to recover depreciation after repairs.

## Step 4: Calculate meaningful deductibles

If the quoted 2% deductible applies to a hypothetical $500,000 insured-value basis, the deductible is **$10,000**. That may be materially different from a $5,000 flat deductible. In another policy, the percentage may use a different specified base. Always inspect how the contract calculates it.

For a hypothetical fully covered $40,000 loss, the simple amount left after a $10,000 deductible is $30,000; after a $5,000 deductible it is $35,000. These calculations do not account for valuation, coinsurance, exclusions or claim adjustments and are not promised payments.

## Step 5: Examine income, equipment and code provisions

Property repair is only one financial problem. If the location cannot reopen, business income or extra expense coverage may matter. Ordinance or law insurance could be relevant when reconstruction must comply with updated building codes. Equipment breakdown might address certain machinery exposures. Mobile tools, customer property, or stock in transit can require separate review.

Check whether the quote includes these protections, whether the limits are sufficient for the described values, and what events trigger them. A feature listed on a summary may have important sublimits, waiting periods, or exclusions in its own endorsement.

## Step 6: Verify lease, loan and certificate conditions

Landlords may ask tenants for coverage on their own contents and general liability; lenders may require specific property insurance terms. A customer or property manager may request a certificate. Compare those documents with the actual insurer forms. TDI explains that certificates do not create protection beyond the policy and are subject to Texas rules.

The fact that a carrier provides a quote does not establish that a lender or landlord's requirements are met. Ask early for approved endorsements and confirm effective dates after binding.

## An El Paso business comparison

*Hypothetical scenario:* An El Paso owner operates a small storefront and receives two quotes. Both show the same building limit, but one has ACV roof provisions while the other has replacement-cost terms with conditions. One includes inventory and a business income section; the other only insures the structure. Before choosing, the owner needs the policy forms, a current inventory, a realistic building valuation, and clarification about wind/hail and water exclusions. Looking only at the annual total would conceal important differences.

## Ten questions for your agent

1. Do both quotes list the same business, address, owner, buildings, and tenants?
2. Do both use accurate rebuilding and contents amounts?
3. What is the cause-of-loss form and what is excluded?
4. Are the roof and building valued in the same way?
5. What is each deductible in actual dollars for a relevant loss?
6. How are wind, hail, water, mold, sewer backup, and flood treated?
7. Is business income/extra expense included, with what trigger and duration?
8. Are equipment breakdown, code upgrades, off-site tools, or special property separately addressed?
9. Do the lease and lender requirements match the actual policy endorsements?
10. What is the full term premium, payment schedule, effective date, and insurer eligibility status?

## Frequently asked questions

### Are two policies with the same building limit equivalent?

No. Valuation, covered causes, exclusions, deductibles, and optional endorsements can differ.

### Is the quote with the lowest premium necessarily the best choice?

Not necessarily. Lower price can accompany higher deductibles or narrower protection. Compare the complete terms.

### Can an independent agency guarantee coverage or approval?

No. The insurer controls underwriting, terms and claim determinations. The agency can help clarify and compare available proposals.
